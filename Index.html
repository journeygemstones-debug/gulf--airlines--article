The Story

When the Middle East's Busiest Skies Went Silent

For decades, Dubai, Doha, and Abu Dhabi have been the world's great crossroads of the skies — the invisible pivot points where a passenger from London might connect to Singapore, or a traveller from Sydney arrive refreshed for a business meeting in New York. The Gulf's "Big Three" — Emirates, Qatar Airways, and Etihad Airways — built an aviation empire on this geographic gift.

Then, on February 28, 2026, the war began. As conflict erupted between Israel, the United States, and Iran, Middle Eastern airspace became a danger zone overnight. Airports closed. Thousands of flights were cancelled in hours. Nearly 1.8 million passengers found themselves stranded, rerouted, or waiting anxiously at departure boards that showed nothing but red.

The impact was unlike anything the region had seen since the COVID-19 pandemic — and in some ways, more concentrated. In March alone, Dubai International Airport — the world's busiest — recorded a 66% collapse in passenger numbers.

Aviation is always impacted by crisis. This is another shock — and what you're seeing is airlines in the Gulf making decisions on their fleet, their schedule, and how they look at their internal structure to re-emerge.

Airline by Airline

How Each Carrier Has Fared

Recovery has been uneven. While all three major carriers were grounded or severely limited in late February and March, they have clawed back capacity at different speeds through April and May 2026.

✈️
Emirates
Capacity Restored~90%

The Dubai-based giant has led the recovery, steadily rebuilding flight numbers week by week. By early May 2026, Emirates was operating close to 90% of its pre-war schedule — though still below its usual 1,250–1,300 daily flights. New Boeing 777X deliveries due late 2026 may be delayed until the market fully stabilises.

✈️
Qatar Airways
Capacity Restored~75%

Qatar has shown strong momentum and is the most aggressive in scheduling future capacity jumps — with a bump planned for May 16 and another for June 16. Doha's position slightly further from the main conflict zone has aided its recovery, and the airline recently secured a major Boeing order as a signal of long-term confidence.

✈️
Etihad Airways
Capacity Restored~72%

Abu Dhabi's flag carrier has faced the highest percentage impact relative to its fleet size. However, flexible rebooking and refund policies extended through May 2026, combined with a 25% reduction in loyalty tier thresholds, have helped retain its most valuable customers through the disruption.

✈️
flydubai & Regional Carriers
Capacity Restored~74%

Smaller carriers and low-cost operators were hit hard and lack the financial cushion of the Big Three. Regional short-haul routes are recovering faster than long-haul international services, but volatile fuel prices and insurance premiums continue to squeeze margins across the board.

The Fuel Crisis

A Cost Shock That Shook the World

Beyond grounding planes, the war delivered a second blow: jet fuel prices roughly doubled. This sent shockwaves far beyond Gulf airspace. Airlines in Europe and Asia that had nothing to do with the conflict found themselves imposing fuel surcharges and trimming routes.

Premium cabin fares surged as available seats became scarce. Some carriers implemented $15–30 surcharges on new bookings within days. Cargo delays cascaded into global supply chains, and the broader energy market remained on edge throughout March and April.

Loyalty Under Pressure

Keeping Frequent Flyers On Side

With their most valuable passengers flying less — or not at all — Gulf carriers moved quickly to protect loyalty relationships. Etihad cut tier qualification thresholds by 25% and automatically promoted some members. Qatar Airways extended status across various Privilege Club tiers.

Industry analysts compared these moves to early COVID-era measures, noting that protecting premium customer relationships now is cheaper than rebuilding them later. Emirates faced pressure to implement similar policies as the months wore on.

⚠️
The Long-Term Brand Risk

For years, Gulf carriers built their dominance on being the world's most reliable hub connectors between Europe and Asia-Pacific. The war has exposed a structural vulnerability in that model. Even after peace returns, passengers and corporate travel managers may diversify away from Gulf hubs — favouring direct routes via northern Europe, Africa, or East Asia. Rebuilding trust in the route network could take anywhere from six months to two years, according to aviation industry veterans.

Key Moments

A Crisis in Chronological Order

Feb 28, 2026
War Begins — Airports Shut Overnight

Israel and the United States launch strikes on Iran. Gulf airports and airspace close in rapid succession. Thousands of flights are cancelled within hours; stranded passengers fill terminals across the region.

Early March 2026
Partial Resumption — Missile Threats Remain

Emirates and Etihad resume limited schedules from the UAE. A chartered Air France evacuation flight is forced to turn back mid-flight due to active missile activity. British repatriation flights begin operating through Oman.

March 2026
Dubai International Logs 66% Passenger Drop

Monthly passenger data confirms the severity of the crisis at the world's busiest airport. Jet fuel prices have doubled. Loyalty programs begin offering emergency status protections to retain premium flyers.

April 2026
UAE Airspace Fully Reopened — Recovery Accelerates

UAE civil aviation authorities lift all capacity restrictions. Dubai Airports CEO Paul Griffiths signals a rapid return to full capacity. Emirates reaches around 80% of pre-war operations; Qatar approaches 60–70%.

May 2026
Emirates Hits ~90% — Full Recovery in Sight

Emirates is close to 90% of pre-war flight numbers. Etihad and Qatar are at 70–80%. Some European and Asian airlines have yet to resume Gulf routes but are expected to do so by late May. No peace deal is yet in place, and the situation remains fluid.

Looking Ahead

Three Scenarios for Gulf Aviation

🟢
Best Case
Conflict ends by Q3 2026

Full capacity restoration by June–July. Passenger confidence rebounds within months. Gulf carriers resume growth trajectories with minimal lasting market-share damage.

🟡
Base Case
Ceasefire holds, no peace

Gradual recovery continues through 2026. Some permanent rerouting by travellers. Gulf hubs remain relevant but face structural competition from alternative corridors. Full recovery takes 12–18 months.

🔴
Worst Case
Conflict re-escalates

Renewed hostilities force airspace closures again. Fuel supplies tighten, forcing airlines to ground planes. Permanent network adjustments and possible consolidation among weaker regional carriers. Recovery pushed to 2027–2028.

Bottom Line

The Skies Will Clear — The Question Is When

Gulf airlines have weathered turbulence before — the 2020 pandemic, geopolitical embargoes, the 2010 volcanic ash crisis. Each time, they have emerged and rebuilt. The current disruption, while severe, is still less globally catastrophic than COVID-19 at its peak. But the concentrated geographic impact on the world's most strategically vital aviation hubs is a stark reminder of how quickly the invisible architecture of modern travel can fracture.

For passengers, the immediate advice is to monitor bookings closely, take advantage of flexible rebooking policies, and consider travel insurance for Gulf-region itineraries. For the airlines themselves, the months ahead will define whether this crisis becomes a footnote or a turning point.

The engines are running. The question is whether the skies will stay open long enough for them to soar again.
